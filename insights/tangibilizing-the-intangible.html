Tangibilizing the intangible.

You cannot market a service the way you market a product. Fifty years on, G. Lynn Shostack's framework is still the clearest explanation of why — and what to do about it instead.

For decades, marketers applied a product-built playbook to services — the four Ps, the brand pyramid, the packaging logic. It didn't work, and for a long time nobody could fully explain why. In 1977, G. Lynn Shostack named the problem directly: squeezing services into the phrase "intangible products" isn't a simplification, it's a contradiction in terms.

The classic marketing mix was built entirely around physical goods — things a customer could inspect, compare, and hold before they paid. Service companies kept borrowing those same tools anyway, because nothing else existed yet. Marketing simply hadn't built a relevant paradigm for something a customer can't touch before they commit to it.

Nothing is purely a product. Nothing is purely a service.

Shostack's molecular model reframes the whole category. Every offering is a molecule of tangible and intangible elements bonded together — what matters is which element is dominant. Salt and soft drinks sit near the tangible end. Teaching, consulting, and airlines sit near the intangible end. Almost nothing is purely one or the other; an airline seat is a physical object, but what you're actually buying is trust in a process you can't see. Your marketing emphasis should follow wherever your specific offering actually lives on that spectrum — not where the category label suggests it should.

A service is rendered. A service is experienced. A service cannot be stored.

Products can be inspected before purchase. Services only exist in the moment of delivery. The consumer has to judge what they cannot yet see — which means, in practice, they judge everything around the service instead: the office, the proposal, the way the phone gets answered. This single fact changes everything about how value gets communicated.

The working model

Proof, process, and people carry the weight a product's packaging would otherwise carry.

01 · Proof

Tangible evidence is your primary marketing tool

Products use abstract association to enhance a physical object. Services do the opposite — concrete, tangible evidence gives shape to an abstract experience: office environment, visual identity, stationery quality, case studies, proposal design, testimonials, even email tone. Shostack's example: a physician's teak-and-leather office versus plastic slipcovers creates an entirely different reality, even when the medical quality is identical.

02 · Process

Make the invisible visible — the process is the product

Because services are intangible, customers have no reference point for quality before delivery. Documenting and communicating your process replaces what a product box would otherwise tell them: define every step and name it, demystify jargon into client-felt outcomes, then differentiate — your process is proprietary IP, worth owning and branding, not hiding.

03 · People

In many fields, the person is perceived to be the service

The consumer often cannot distinguish between the service and the human delivering it — a hard marketing variable, not a soft HR concern. Shostack's Citibank case: replacing portfolio managers with salesmen failed, because clients hadn't bought "investment management," they'd bought their specific manager. IBM, McDonald's, and airlines used uniform dress codes for the same reason — each an unspoken signal of reliability.

"Squeezing services into the phrase 'intangible products' is not only a distortion — it is a complete contradiction in terms."

Where this shows up in your business.

Your team's presence, language, credentials, and consistency are marketing, whether you manage them deliberately or not. So is your office, your proposal design, and the visible shape of how you actually deliver. None of that is decoration around the "real" service — for anything intangible-dominant, it is the service, as far as the customer can perceive it before they commit.

The practical test is the same one Shostack proposed in 1977 and it still holds: the more intangible your offering, the more your marketing has to lean on tangible proof. If a prospective client can't yet experience your work, what can they see, touch, or read that stands in for it honestly?
